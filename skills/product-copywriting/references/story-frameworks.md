# Story frameworks

Pick one frame per page or flow. Write the one-sentence story first; if you can't, the brief is missing something.

| Frame | Best for | Length |
|---|---|---|
| [Customer-as-hero arc](#1-customer-as-hero-arc) | Homepage, product landing page, pitch | Whole page |
| [PAS](#2-pas-problem--agitate--solve) | Ads, cold email, a single section, problem-aware readers | 3-6 sentences |
| [BAB](#3-bab-before--after--bridge) | Feature sections, onboarding intros, case-study summaries | 3-5 sentences |
| [AIDA](#4-aida-attention--interest--desire--action) | Long sales pages, launch emails, store descriptions | Whole page |
| [Origin story](#5-origin-story) | About page, founder letter, brand video script | 150-500 words |
| [Day in the life](#6-day-in-the-life) | Feature pages, onboarding, use-case pages | 80-250 words |
| [Jobs-to-be-done](#7-jobs-to-be-done-statements) | Positioning, hero headlines, research synthesis | 1 sentence each |

---

## 1. Customer-as-hero arc

Inspired by the classic hero's-journey structure. The reader is the hero. Your product is the guide who has seen this problem before and hands them a plan. Most weak homepages fail because the company casts itself as the hero.

**When to use:** any page whose job is to explain what you do and why it matters, to someone who doesn't know you yet.

**Structure (one beat per section):**

| Beat | Question it answers | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| Character | Who is this for, and what do they want? | Eyebrow, H1 |
| Problem: external | What is in their way, visibly? ("Invoices go out late") | Subhead, problem section |
| Problem: internal | How does that make them feel? ("Like a nag, not a designer") | Problem section |
| Problem: philosophical | Why is that wrong? ("Doing the work should be enough to get paid") | One line, often the About page |
| Guide: empathy | Show you understand: "We've chased late payments too." | Problem → solution bridge |
| Guide: authority | Show you can help: real numbers, logos, quotes (only if provided) | Social proof |
| Plan | 3 simple steps that make it feel doable | How it works |
| Call to action | One direct ask, plus a softer one for people not ready yet | Hero CTA, final CTA |
| Stakes | What happens if nothing changes (kept honest, never scary) | Problem section, final CTA |
| Success | What life looks like after, in concrete terms | Benefits, testimonials, final CTA |

**One-sentence story:** "[Reader] wants [goal] but [problem]. [Product] helps by [plan], so they get [success] and avoid [stakes]."

**Before (company as hero)**
> We are a leading invoicing platform built with cutting-edge technology to empower freelancers.

**After (customer as hero)**
> You finished the project three weeks ago. You're still waiting to get paid.
> Send the invoice the day the work is done, and we'll send the polite reminders for you.

## 2. PAS: Problem → Agitate → Solve

Name the problem, make it felt with one specific detail, then offer the way out.

**When to use:** the reader already knows they have the problem. Short formats: ads, email, a single landing-page section.

**Structure:**
1. **Problem**: one sentence, in the reader's words.
2. **Agitate**: one or two concrete consequences. Stay honest; no doom.
3. **Solve**: what you offer and the first step.

**Before**
> Managing your team's schedules can be challenging. Our solution streamlines scheduling.

**After**
> Someone always misses the shift change. Then you're texting five people at 6am to find cover.
> Shiftly posts open shifts to the whole team and fills them before your coffee's done.

Agitate with facts, not fear. If the "agitate" line would make a reader anxious about health, money or safety, cut it back.

## 3. BAB: Before → After → Bridge

Show the reader's world now, show it after, then show how to get there.

**When to use:** features with an obvious transformation; onboarding intros; the summary line of a case study.

**Structure:**
1. **Before**: the current, specific frustration.
2. **After**: the same scene, fixed.
3. **Bridge**: the product, and how it gets them across.

**Before**
> Our reporting tool provides comprehensive insights for data-driven decisions.

**After**
> Before: Monday mornings go to copying numbers from four tools into one slide.
> After: The slide is already in your inbox at 8am.
> Bridge: Connect your tools once. Reports build themselves every week.

On a real page drop the "Before/After" labels and keep the shape.

## 4. AIDA: Attention → Interest → Desire → Action

The old direct-response sequence. Useful as a page skeleton; pair it with the hero arc so it doesn't turn into a pitch.

**When to use:** long sales pages, launch announcements, app store descriptions.

**Structure:**

| Step | Job | Typical section |
|---|---|---|
| Attention | Stop the scroll with the reader's outcome or problem | H1 + subhead |
| Interest | Show you understand their situation; add one surprising fact you can back up | Problem, how it works |
| Desire | Make the after-state concrete; show proof | Benefits, testimonials, demo |
| Action | One clear next step; remove the last worry | CTA + risk reducer ("Cancel anytime") |

**Before**
> Introducing TaskFlow! The ultimate productivity solution! Sign up now!

**After**
> **Attention:** Your to-do list has 43 items. Three of them matter today.
> **Interest:** TaskFlow reads your calendar and deadlines, then shows the three.
> **Desire:** [placeholder: customer quote about finishing the important work first]
> **Action:** Try it free for 14 days. No card needed.

## 5. Origin story

Why this company exists, told as a short, true story. The founder is a character here, but the reader should still see their own problem in it.

**When to use:** About page, founder letter, careers page intro, brand video.

**Structure:**
1. **The moment**: a specific scene where the problem hit (time, place, what broke).
2. **The frustration**: what existing options got wrong.
3. **The decision**: what you set out to do differently.
4. **The belief**: one line on what you think should be true.
5. **Now**: who you serve today and what has changed for them (real facts only).
6. **Invitation**: where the reader fits in.

**Rules:** only true details from the user. If you don't have them, write the frame with `[placeholder: the moment the founder hit the problem]` and ask. Don't invent garages, dorm rooms or napkin sketches.

**Before**
> Founded in 2019, we are passionate about revolutionizing the way people manage their finances.

**After**
> In 2019, Priya spent a weekend rebuilding her parents' budget from a shoebox of receipts. Every app she tried assumed you already knew what an "expense category" was.
> So she built one that starts with a photo of a receipt and a single question: "What was this for?"
> Today it's used by [placeholder: number of households, if known] families who never wanted to learn accounting.

## 6. Day in the life

Walk the reader through a real day (or task) with the product in it. Readers picture themselves faster than they process feature lists.

**When to use:** feature pages, use-case pages, onboarding carousels, demo video scripts.

**Structure:**
1. Start at a specific time or moment ("8:40, coffee in hand").
2. Show 2-4 moments where the product does its job, each tied to one feature.
3. End on the result the reader cares about ("Home by six").

**Before**
> Features include automated reminders, real-time sync, mobile access and smart reports.

**After**
> 8:40. You open the app and see two clients paid overnight.
> 11:15. A reminder went out to the one who didn't. You didn't write it.
> 4:30. You send the last invoice from your phone, on the train home.

Keep it plausible. Use the reader's role, not a stock persona with an invented name, unless the user provides one.

## 7. Jobs-to-be-done statements

A way to find what to say before you write the headline. People "hire" a product to make progress in a situation.

**When to use:** positioning, choosing a hero headline, turning research or reviews into copy.

**Structure:**
> When **[situation]**, I want to **[motivation]**, so I can **[expected outcome]**.

Write 3-5 of these from what the user tells you (reviews, tickets, interviews). The headline usually comes from the outcome; the subhead from the situation.

**Example**
- When *a client pays late*, I want to *nudge them without sounding desperate*, so I can *get paid and keep the relationship*.
- Headline from it: **"Get paid without the awkward follow-up"**

**Before (feature-first)**
> Automated payment reminder workflows with customizable templates.

**After (job-first)**
> Late payers get a friendly reminder on day 3, day 7 and day 14. You never have to write one.

---

## Choosing and combining

- One frame per page. Sections can use smaller frames inside it (a PAS problem section inside a hero-arc homepage).
- If the reader doesn't know they have the problem, start with the problem (PAS, hero arc). If they do and are comparing, lead with the result and the proof (BAB, AIDA).
- Apps: onboarding is a short BAB (before → after → first step). Empty states are a one-line plan. Errors are not stories; keep them plain.
